What is Volume Price Analysis?

Every bar on a chart is a record of a battle. Price spread tells you the result, volume tells you the effort, and the close position within the bar tells you who won. VPA (rooted in Wyckoff, popularised by Tom Williams and Anna Coulling) reads these three together to infer what large operators — "smart money" — are doing: accumulating, distributing, or standing aside.

The master principle is effort vs result. Big effort (volume) should produce a big result (spread and progress). When it doesn't — huge volume, tiny movement — someone is absorbing the other side, and the chart is telling you a turn or a trap is forming. Every signal below is a variation of this one idea.

Reversal warning

Climactic volume (buying / selling climax)

What to look for: after an extended move, a very wide-spread bar on volume 2.5×+ its recent average — often gapping, often making the news.

What it means: the crowd finally piles in… and professionals sell to them (or in a down climax, panic sellers dump into professional buying). Maximum effort at the very end of a move is a hallmark of excess.

What usually follows: momentum stalls within a few bars; reversals or long consolidations are common. Never chase a climax bar.

Demand appears

Stopping volume

What to look for: in a downtrend, a bar that undercuts the prior low on heavy volume but closes in the upper part of its range — a long tail underneath.

What it means: all that selling was met — absorbed — by willing buyers. The supply hitting the market found demand strong enough to push the close back up. This is professional accumulation in action.

What usually follows: the decline stalls. Expect basing, a test on lower volume, then often a markup. The low of the stopping bar becomes key support.

Rally in trouble

No demand

What to look for: after a rally, an up-bar with a narrow spread whose volume is lower than the previous two bars.

What it means: price is drifting up but nobody with size is buying. Professionals aren't interested at these prices — the rally is running on fumes.

What usually follows: stall or rollover, especially if it appears at resistance or after a climax. If it appears during a healthy uptrend's pause, wait for confirmation before shorting.

Trend continuation

No supply

What to look for: inside an uptrend pullback, a narrow down-bar on volume lower than the previous two bars.

What it means: the dip is happening on no selling pressure — holders aren't dumping, and there's no supply to absorb. The path of least resistance remains up.

What usually follows: one of the most reliable continuation signals. When the next bar turns up, the uptrend typically resumes. This is the quiet pullback breakout traders wait to buy.

Battle at a level

Effort vs result (absorption)

What to look for: massive volume — 2.5×+ average — on a bar with a tiny range, usually at a key level or after a strong push.

What it means: enormous effort produced no progress. Every buyer met a seller of equal size (or vice versa). One side is quietly absorbing everything the other throws at it.

What usually follows: resolution — often against the prior thrust. The absorbing side reveals itself in the next few bars. Don't predict; watch which way price breaks from the absorption bar's range.

Setup anatomy

Breakout anatomy (Breakout Judge)

The setups the game surfaces follow the structure behind history's biggest movers: a prior uptrend (the stock has already proven demand), then a tight base — a multi-week consolidation where range contracts and volume dries up (supply exhausted) — then a breakout bar clearing the base high on expanding volume.

  • Good breakouts: tight base, volume dry-up before, range + volume expansion on the day, close near the high.
  • Bad breakouts: loose sloppy base, breakout closing mid-range, weak volume, or a late-stage base after the move is already extended.
The real skill

Risk management & R-multiples (Trade Manager)

Calling direction is a fraction of trading. The rest is what you do after entry — and that's what Trade Manager drills, candle by candle.

  • 1R = your initial risk — the distance from entry to stop. All results are measured in R: risking $1 to make $3 is a +3R trade. A system that wins only 40% of the time but averages +2R per winner is highly profitable.
  • Set the stop where the trade is wrong — below the structure (swing low, base low, stopping-volume bar low), not at an arbitrary % you're comfortable losing.
  • Manage bar by bar: as each candle closes, re-ask: is the reason I entered still true? Trail your stop under new higher lows (for longs) or move to breakeven once you have 1R+ of cushion.
  • Never widen a stop. Moving a stop away from price converts a small planned loss into an unplanned disaster. The game gives 0 XP and flags it — because in real life it's how accounts die.
  • A stopped-out 1R loss executed cleanly is a good trade. The game pays XP for it. Outcome ≠ process: judge yourself on execution, and the outcomes follow over hundreds of reps.
